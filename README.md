## Overview

Anansi (Akan: Ananse) is a spider trickster figure of Akan oral tradition, especially associated with the Ashanti people of present-day Ghana. The name itself means 'spider' in Akan, and Anansi is variously depicted as a literal spider, an anthropomorphic spider with a human face, or a human-shaped figure with spider-like features. Anansi is the central character of a vast cycle of folktales known in Akan as Anansesem ('Spider Stories'). Two foundational Anansi tales are widely retold: in one, Anansi acquires all stories from the sky-god Nyame by completing a series of impossible tasks, becoming the owner of all stories; in another, Anansi tries to hoard all wisdom in a clay pot, only to break it and disperse wisdom across the world. The Atlantic slave trade carried Anansi narratives to the Caribbean and the Americas, where he survived under names such as Anancy (Jamaica), Aunt Nancy (American South), Kompa Nanzi (Curaçao), and Bro Nansi. In the diaspora, Anansi tales served as resistance narratives that valorised the cleverness of the apparently weak. Anansi has been the subject of substantial 20th- and 21st-century literary adaptation, including Neil Gaiman's 'American Gods' (2001) and 'Anansi Boys' (2005), and remains a living oral tradition in West Africa and the Caribbean.
